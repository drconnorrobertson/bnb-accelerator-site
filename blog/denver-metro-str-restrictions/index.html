Denver: Why the Investable Deals Are Not Inside City Limits

Published March 20, 2026·6 min read

A $540,000 property on Cottonwood Drive in Denver with a total entry of $185,619 is one of the more accessible entry points in our deal inventory. Understanding why it works requires understanding what Denver's rules actually restrict and what they do not.

The primary residence rule

Denver requires a short-term rental license tied to the host's primary residence. A licensee must demonstrate that the property is their primary residence, and the license permits rental of that residence rather than of investment property held for the purpose.

The practical effect is that conventional non-owner-occupied investment purchases are excluded within Denver city limits. This is not a soft restriction or a permitting queue. It is a structural exclusion.

For an investor, that means Denver proper is off the table for the standard model, and any property marketed as a Denver short-term rental investment deserves careful verification of exactly where the parcel sits and what license it holds.

Where the metro deals are

The Denver metropolitan area extends well beyond Denver city and county, and the surrounding jurisdictions regulate independently. Adams, Arapahoe, Jefferson and Douglas counties, and the municipalities within them, each set their own rules.

Some of those jurisdictions permit non-owner-occupied short-term rentals with registration. Others restrict similarly to Denver. The variation is genuine and it is parcel-specific, which is why the address matters more here than the metro name.

The Cottonwood Drive property carries a Denver mailing address, which illustrates the point precisely: postal addresses in the metro do not reliably indicate the governing jurisdiction. Establishing which authority controls the parcel is the first step, not an afterthought.

Never assume the governing jurisdiction from the mailing address in a large metro. Confirm the parcel's municipality and county with the assessor before relying on any regulatory analysis.

What Denver metro demand supports

Denver has a substantial business travel base, a major convention center, a large medical complex, professional sports across four leagues, and an outdoor recreation identity that draws leisure travel year round.

That mix produces a much flatter annual curve than a mountain resort market. Urban demand comes from business, medical, event, family and leisure travel, and those segments do not peak together, which is generally good for debt service coverage.

It is also a gateway market. A meaningful share of Denver-area stays are travelers positioning for a mountain trip, which adds weekend and seasonal demand on top of the urban base.

The Colorado risk to watch

Colorado has debated changing the property tax classification of short-term rentals toward a commercial rate. If that happened, it would materially raise operating costs on every affected property in the state.

This is a live risk rather than a theoretical one, and it belongs in any Colorado underwriting as a scenario rather than a footnote. Model the property with a materially higher property tax line and confirm it still works.

The mountain resort towns have moved separately, with Summit County and Steamboat Springs adopting caps and overlay restrictions. The direction of travel across Colorado has been toward restriction, which is the general context for any purchase in the state.

How we approach the Front Range

Selectively. Colorado is not one of our high-volume markets precisely because the regulatory direction has been unfavorable and the property tax question is unresolved.

Where we do transact, the discipline is the same as everywhere: establish the governing jurisdiction from the parcel, confirm the use is permitted and the license is obtainable and transferable, read the association declaration, and model the long-term rental floor in case the rules tighten.

The mountain alternative

Investors drawn to Colorado for the mountain product rather than the metro face a different set of constraints, and they are generally tighter.

Summit County and its towns operate license caps and zone-based license classes. Steamboat Springs has adopted overlay restrictions. Several other resort communities have moved similarly. The direction of travel across Colorado's resort economy has been toward restriction for several years, driven by workforce housing pressure that is genuine and not going away.

For an investor who wants ski market exposure, Utah and Idaho currently offer better regulatory positions. Park City permits nightly rental in defined zones, and Idaho code prevents local governments from prohibiting short-term rentals outright, which protects McCall, Sun Valley and Coeur d'Alene. Both markets have real demand and neither carries Colorado's unresolved property tax classification question. That is not a reason to rule Colorado out, but it is a reason to compare rather than assume.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy an investment short-term rental in Denver?

Not within Denver city limits under the standard model. Denver ties short-term rental licensing to the host's primary residence, which structurally excludes non-owner-occupied investment purchases.

Are there short-term rental opportunities in the Denver metro?

In surrounding jurisdictions, sometimes. Adams, Arapahoe, Jefferson and Douglas counties and their municipalities regulate independently, and some permit non-owner-occupied rentals with registration. Verify the governing jurisdiction from the parcel rather than the mailing address.

What is the property tax risk in Colorado?

The state has debated reclassifying short-term rentals toward a commercial property tax rate, which would materially raise operating costs. Model a higher property tax line as a scenario in any Colorado underwriting.
