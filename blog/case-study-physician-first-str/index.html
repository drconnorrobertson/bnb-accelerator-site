Case Study: A Physician Couple's First Short-Term Rental

Published July 9, 2026·7 min read

A physician couple in the Midwest, roughly $680,000 of combined W-2 income, no prior real estate beyond a primary residence, and about eleven hours a week between them. Here is how their first acquisition actually ran, from the first call in June to a live listing in the same tax year.

The starting position

They had been told repeatedly that real estate was the answer to a large tax bill, had bought a duplex three years earlier on that advice, and had watched the paper loss suspend and carry forward doing nothing. That is exactly what Section 469 does with a passive loss when there is no passive income to absorb it, and neither of them could plausibly qualify as a real estate professional at 55 clinical hours a week.

The duplex was not a bad property. It was the wrong instrument for the problem they were solving. See the comparison of both routes.

The constraint that shaped everything

Their CPA wanted the property placed in service before December 31 to take the deduction against the current year. That single requirement determined the market, the property type, and the entire timeline.

A ground up build was out. A heavy renovation was out. What was in: an existing, functional home in a market with available inventory and a vendor bench deep enough to furnish and launch in weeks rather than months. See why the placed-in-service date governs.

Market and property selection

The Sevierville and Pigeon Forge corridor, for four reasons: proven multi season demand, no state income tax in Tennessee, a deep operator bench, and a cabin inventory profile that supports both large group revenue and heavy short life property for cost segregation.

They bought a seven bedroom cabin with a game room, hot tub, and genuine mountain view. Purchase price landed at $1.02 million after negotiation from a $1.135 million ask.

The two decisions that made the tax position work

Management structure. They used a co-host rather than a full service manager. The co-host handles turnovers, physical issues, and vendor coordination on the ground. The couple retained pricing, calendar, and guest communication. That split is what kept the 100 hour material participation test achievable, since a full service manager's hours would have counted against them. See co-hosting versus self managing.

Booking policy. They set a minimum stay policy designed to keep the annual average period of customer use comfortably under seven days. Average stay came in at 3.6 nights. That is the test under Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A), and it is the reason this property was treated differently from their duplex. See the seven day rule explained.

What the participation looked like in practice

They logged 148 documented hours between them across the partial year, contemporaneously, with specific descriptions and supporting evidence: platform message timestamps, vendor texts, purchase receipts, and two trips to the property. Not a reconstructed spreadsheet with round numbers. See how to keep a defensible log.

The outcome

A cost segregation study performed by an independent firm reclassified a substantial share of the depreciable basis into five, seven, and fifteen year categories. The couple's CPA applied the applicable bonus depreciation percentage for their placed-in-service year and used the resulting loss against ordinary income. The first year tax reduction was the largest single line item in their financial year, and the property produced positive cash flow from month two.

The specifics of their deduction are theirs and depend entirely on their facts, their marginal rates, and the bonus percentage in effect. What is generalizable is the sequence: the right property type, in a market that supports short average stays, with a management structure that preserves participation, placed in service before the deadline. Miss any one of those and the outcome changes materially.

They bought a second property fourteen months later. That is the more common outcome than most people expect, and it is why we plan the second acquisition while the first is still in escrow. See scaling from one to ten and our partner firm's material on short-term rental tax strategy.

Frequently asked questions

Why did the couple's duplex not reduce their tax bill?

Rental real estate is a passive activity under Section 469, so a paper loss from a duplex can only offset passive income. With no passive income and no realistic path to real estate professional status at 55 clinical hours a week, the loss suspended and carried forward without producing any current benefit.

How long did the acquisition take?

From first call in June to a live listing in the same tax year. Meeting a December 31 placed-in-service deadline required an existing functional home in a market with available inventory and enough vendor capacity to furnish and launch in weeks, which ruled out builds and heavy renovations.

Why did they use a co-host instead of a property manager?

To preserve material participation. A full service manager's hours count against the test requiring that no other individual participates more than the owner. With a co-host handling turnovers and physical issues while the owners retained pricing, calendar, and guest communication, the 100 hour test stayed achievable.

Are these results typical?

No. This is a composite drawn from completed acquisitions with details changed, and figures are illustrative rather than promised. Outcomes depend on the property, the market, management structure, marginal tax rates, and the bonus depreciation percentage in effect for the placed-in-service year.
