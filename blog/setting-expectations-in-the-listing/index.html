Setting Expectations in the Listing to Protect the Review

Published November 26, 2025·6 min read

Guests do not rate a property against other properties. They rate it against what they expected, which means the listing sets the standard the review is measured against, and an oversold listing guarantees disappointment regardless of how good the property is.

The mechanism

A modest, spotless, accurately described property with easy check-in consistently outperforms an expensive one that overpromised. That is not intuitive and it is well established across the industry.

The reason is that satisfaction is the gap between expectation and experience. Raising the expectation without raising the experience narrows or reverses the gap.

This is good news operationally, because it means review performance is controllable through writing rather than through capital.

Disclose the friction

  • A steep or gravel driveway, particularly if a low vehicle would struggle.
  • Stairs, especially if there is no ground-floor bedroom.
  • Distance to the beach, lift or attraction, stated honestly in walking minutes.
  • Road noise, train lines or nearby construction.
  • Shared amenities where guests might expect private ones.
  • Seasonal limitations, such as a pool that is not heated or a road that requires four-wheel drive in winter.

Each of these costs some bookings at the margin. Each of them costs far more in reviews if discovered on arrival, and the review cost persists.

Photography honesty

Excessively wide lenses make rooms look substantially larger than they are, which produces arrival disappointment that guests frequently cannot articulate. The stay felt off and they do not know why.

The standard is a photograph a guest standing in the room recognizes. Slightly flattering is normal; misleading is a review problem.

The same applies to cropping. Framing to exclude the neighboring building or the utility area is technically accurate and practically misleading, and the guest finds out at the door.

Being specific about capacity

State how many the property sleeps and how, bed by bed. State how many it seats at the dining table. State how many bathrooms and where they are.

Group organizers booking for ten adults need those numbers to make a decision, and a listing that omits them either loses the booking or produces a guest who arrives with expectations the property does not meet.

Captions are the best place for this, because they attach the information to the photograph of the room being discussed and most guests read captions even when they skip the description.

Under-promise on the extras

Where a property has genuinely good features, describing them plainly rather than superlatively leaves room for the guest to be impressed. 'Fast wifi, tested at 300 down' is more credible and more satisfying than 'blazing fast wifi'.

The same applies to views, hot tubs and kitchens. A specific, modest claim that the property exceeds performs better than a superlative it merely meets.

It also protects against the compounding problem: superlatives raise expectations for everything, so a property described as luxurious is judged as luxurious in every room including the fourth bedroom.

The cancellation math

Owners resist disclosure because it costs bookings. That is true and the arithmetic favors disclosure anyway.

A guest who reads about the gravel driveway and books elsewhere costs you a night you can resell. A guest who arrives and is unhappy about the driveway costs you a review, and a review affects bookings for months.

The trade is a marginal booking now against a persistent ranking effect later. In the first year, when the review denominator is small, that trade is not close.

Reviewing your own listing

Read the listing as a guest arriving for the first time and ask what would surprise them. Then read your reviews and look for language suggesting a mismatch: 'not quite what we expected', 'the photos made it look bigger', 'we did not realise'.

Those phrases are the listing telling you where it oversells. Each one is fixable with a sentence.

Do this after any refresh too. A listing describing furniture that has been replaced or an amenity that has been removed is inaccurate in a way that is easy to miss and easy to fix.

The pre-arrival reinforcement

Disclosure in the listing is step one. Repeating the important items in the pre-arrival message is step two, and it catches the guests who skimmed.

The items worth repeating are the ones that produce arrival problems: the driveway, the stairs, the parking arrangement, the walk to the beach or lift. A sentence in the day-before message costs nothing and eliminates the surprise.

It also creates a record. A guest who complains about a steep driveway after being told about it twice is in a weaker position, and platform mediation looks at what was disclosed and when.

The same logic applies to seasonal items. A pool that is not heated in March, a road that needs four-wheel drive in January, a trail that closes in winter. Guests booking six months ahead do not remember what the listing said about a season they were not thinking about.

Frequently asked questions

Should I disclose problems in my listing?

Yes. Guests rate against expectations, so a steep driveway, stairs, real distance to the beach or road noise discovered on arrival costs a review. A guest who reads it and books elsewhere costs a night you can resell.

How wide should listing photos be?

Wide enough to show the space, not so wide that a guest standing in the room would not recognize it. Excessive wide angles produce arrival disappointment that guests often cannot articulate but that shows up in reviews.

What listing language predicts bad reviews?

Superlatives. A property described as luxurious is judged as luxurious in every room. Specific modest claims the property exceeds perform better than superlatives it merely meets.
