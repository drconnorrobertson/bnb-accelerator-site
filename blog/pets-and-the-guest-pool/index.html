Allowing Pets: What It Adds and What It Costs

Published December 27, 2021·6 min read

Allowing pets is one of the few binary decisions that meaningfully changes how many guests can book your property. It also changes cleaning, wear and allergy management, and the trade differs sharply by market.

The size of the guest pool

A large share of American households own a pet, and a meaningful subset of travelers will only book pet-friendly accommodation because boarding is expensive and inconvenient.

Filtering for pet-friendly substantially reduces the available inventory in most markets, which means a pet-friendly property competes in a much less crowded set. That is a genuine advantage in supplied markets.

It also tends to extend length of stay. Guests traveling with a dog frequently drive rather than fly and stay longer, which improves turnover economics.

Where it works best

  • Mountain and cabin markets, where dogs are part of the trip and hiking is the activity.
  • Lake markets, for the same reason.
  • Drive markets generally, since bringing a pet is far easier by car than by air.
  • Rural properties with land, where a fenced area is a genuine differentiator.
  • Shoulder and off seasons, where pet-friendly inventory is even scarcer.

It works less well in premium beach properties with white furnishings, urban rentals with close neighbors and shared entries, and any property where allergy sensitivity in the guest base is high.

The real costs

Cleaning takes longer and requires different work: hair removal, odor treatment, and more thorough attention to soft surfaces. A pet-friendly turnover is genuinely more expensive and the fee should reflect it.

Wear increases on flooring, doors, furniture legs and yard. Hard-surface flooring is close to mandatory, and it is a reason not to make the decision after installing carpet.

Allergy management is the hidden cost. Guests with allergies who book a property that has hosted dogs, even weeks earlier, can have a genuinely bad stay, and it will appear in the review.

Controlling the downside

  1. Charge a pet fee that covers the additional cleaning honestly rather than as a token.
  2. Set clear rules: which areas are off limits, whether pets can be left unattended, crating expectations.
  3. Limit by number and size, stated explicitly rather than left to the guest's judgment.
  4. Hard-surface flooring in all traffic areas and washable covers on primary seating.
  5. Disclose in the listing that pets are welcome, so allergy-sensitive guests can self-select out.
  6. Provide pet amenities: bowls, a bed, towels, waste bags. Cheap and consistently mentioned in reviews.

The fenced yard multiplier

For a property with land, a fenced area is the single highest-return pet-related investment. It moves the property from pet-tolerated to pet-ideal, and guests searching specifically for it will pay for it.

It is also a differentiator that very few competitors have, because fencing is a capital item most owners do not consider. In cabin and lake markets it can be a genuine positioning advantage.

Photograph it and state it explicitly. Guests filtering for pet-friendly are looking for exactly this, and a property that has it and does not mention it is invisible to them.

Service animals are different

Service animals are generally not pets for legal purposes, and policies restricting pets typically do not apply to them. Pet fees generally cannot be charged for service animals.

This is a legal question with real consequences and it varies by jurisdiction and platform policy. A property that is not pet-friendly may still be required to accommodate a service animal.

Understand the distinction before setting policy, and take advice rather than relying on a general summary. This is orientation, not legal advice.

Making the decision

The honest test is whether your market has pet demand and whether your property can absorb the wear. A mountain cabin with hard floors and land is a straightforward yes. A white-furnished 30A beach house with carpet is a straightforward no.

Where it is marginal, consider trialing it for a shoulder season. Pet-friendly inventory is scarcest then, and it is a low-risk window to test whether the additional bookings justify the additional cleaning cost.

The decision is also easier to make before furnishing than after. Flooring and upholstery choices that support pets cost the same as ones that do not, and retrofitting is expensive.

The allergy problem, handled

The genuine cost of allowing pets is not cleaning, it is that a guest with allergies booking weeks later can have a bad stay in a property that hosted a dog.

Two things reduce it. Disclosing clearly in the listing that pets are welcome, so allergy-sensitive guests can self-select out, and a cleaning protocol that includes soft-surface treatment rather than only visible hair removal.

The disclosure is the more important of the two. A guest who knew and booked anyway has a different experience from one who arrives and discovers it, which is the same expectations mechanism that governs every other review outcome.

Some owners run a pet-free period each year, blocking a stretch and deep cleaning it, then marketing that window to allergy-sensitive guests. It works in markets with enough demand to fill the window and it is more effort than most single-property owners want.

Frequently asked questions

Should my short-term rental allow pets?

It depends on the market and the property. Mountain, lake, rural and drive markets have strong pet demand and pet-friendly inventory is scarce. Premium beach properties with soft furnishings and urban rentals with shared entries generally should not.

How much should I charge as a pet fee?

Enough to cover the genuinely higher cleaning cost, which includes hair removal, odor treatment and more thorough attention to soft surfaces. A token fee that does not cover the work is a subsidy.

Are service animals treated as pets?

Generally no. Service animals are typically not pets for legal purposes, pet restrictions usually do not apply to them, and pet fees generally cannot be charged. This varies by jurisdiction and platform policy, so take advice.
