The Cleaning Fee Problem and How to Actually Solve It

Published March 11, 2021·6 min read

The cleaning fee is the most complained-about line item in short-term rental booking, and most owners solve it in the wrong direction. The real problem is rarely the fee itself; it is the ratio between the fee and the length of stay.

Why guests react to it

A $200 cleaning fee on a seven-night stay is $29 a night, which nobody notices. The same fee on a two-night stay is $100 a night on top of the rate, which everybody notices.

Booking platforms display the total, so the guest sees the fee regardless. What changes is whether it reads as reasonable or as a surcharge, and that is entirely a function of the stay length it is spread across.

This is why the loudest cleaning fee complaints come from short-stay markets and weekend properties, and why beach houses booking weekly rarely hear about it.

The wrong solutions

Cutting the fee below your actual cleaning cost means subsidizing turnovers out of your nightly rate, which is fine as a presentation decision and terrible if you did not model it. The cost does not disappear; it moves.

Rolling the entire fee into the nightly rate makes short stays look expensive and long stays look cheap, which inverts the incentive you want. You end up attracting exactly the short bookings that cost the most to service.

Reducing cleaning quality to reduce cost is the most expensive option of all, because cleanliness is the most frequently cited factor in negative reviews and reviews compound.

The actual solution

Change the stay length. A two-night minimum instead of one, or a three-night minimum on peak weekends, spreads the same fee across more nights and makes it disappear as an objection.

It also reduces the number of turnovers, which reduces total cleaning cost, which reduces wear, which reduces the operational load. The fee problem and the profitability problem have the same solution.

  • Set minimums that reflect the market: two nights minimum in most weekend markets, three on peak weekends, five to seven in beach markets during summer.
  • Partially absorb the fee into the nightly rate so the displayed fee looks proportionate, then price the rate accordingly.
  • Use length-of-stay discounts to actively reward the bookings that cost less to service.
  • Never price below variable cost per booking, which includes cleaning net of the fee, consumables and commission.

Knowing your real cleaning cost

Most owners underestimate it, because they think of the cleaner's invoice and not the full picture.

The real per-turnover cost includes the clean itself, linens and laundry, consumables replaced each stay, the inspection or quality check, and the amortized cost of items that get damaged or taken. In a large property with a hot tub or pool, it also includes the between-stay service on those.

Calculate it properly once, per property. That number is your floor and it is the basis for both the fee and the minimum stay decision.

Peak season is different

In a market's peak weeks, demand exceeds supply and the cleaning fee is not the deciding factor in whether a guest books. Longer minimums are easy to enforce and turnover cost per night falls naturally.

In the shoulder and off season, the fee becomes a much larger share of a smaller total and guests are more price-sensitive. That is when a high fee suppresses bookings most.

A reasonable approach is a fee that stays constant and a minimum stay that flexes: longer minimums in peak, shorter in shoulder with a partially absorbed fee to keep the displayed total competitive.

The cleaner relationship

None of this works without a reliable cleaner, and cleaner reliability is the single most common operational failure in short-term rentals.

Depth on the bench matters more than the rate. A cleaner who is 15% cheaper and has no backup will eventually cause a same-day turnover failure, which in peak season is a cancellation, a refund and a review.

Pay a rate that keeps a good team engaged, use photographic checklists so quality is verifiable rather than assumed, and confirm what happens when someone is sick before it happens rather than after.

The competitive check

Before setting a fee, look at what your ten closest comparables charge and, more importantly, what their minimum stays are. Those two numbers move together, and a property with a high fee and a one-night minimum is the one guests complain about.

If your comparables all run a $250 fee with a three-night minimum and you run $250 with a one-night minimum, you are the expensive option in every search where a guest is looking at short stays, and the cheapest option for nobody.

Also check whether the market's fees are trending. In several markets, platforms surfacing total price more prominently has pushed operators toward absorbing more of the fee into the rate. Whether that is right for you depends on your stay length mix, but being significantly out of step with the comparable set on displayed total is a booking problem regardless of the underlying economics.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I charge for cleaning on Airbnb?

Enough to cover your true per-turnover cost, which includes the clean, linens and laundry, consumables, quality inspection and amortized damage. Then solve the guest perception problem with minimum stay length rather than by cutting the fee below cost.

Should I include the cleaning fee in the nightly rate?

Rolling it in entirely makes short stays look expensive and long stays cheap, which attracts the bookings that cost most to service. Partial absorption combined with sensible minimum stays generally works better.

Why do guests complain about cleaning fees?

Because of the ratio to stay length. A $200 fee on seven nights is $29 a night and unnoticed; on two nights it is $100 a night and prominent. The complaint is about short stays, not about the fee.
