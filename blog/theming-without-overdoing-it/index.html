Theming a Rental Without Making It Unbookable

Published October 3, 2022·6 min read

Theming is one of the few design levers that meaningfully differentiates a property in a crowded market, and one of the few that can also make a property unbookable. The difference is whether the theme serves the guest or serves the owner.

Where theming genuinely works

In heavily supplied markets with near-identical inventory. The Orlando corridor is the clearest case: thousands of production-built houses competing for the same guest, where a themed children's bedroom is frequently the difference between the middle and the bottom of the pricing pack.

It also works where the theme matches an existing trip motivation. A Disney-adjacent property with themed bunk rooms is serving the reason the family came. A ski property with a mountain aesthetic is doing the same.

The test is whether the theme makes the property more suitable for the trip the guest is already taking, or merely more distinctive.

Where it fails

When the theme narrows the guest pool without a corresponding rate benefit. A heavily themed property serves fewer guest types, and in a market with thin demand that is a straightforward loss.

When it expresses the owner's taste rather than the market's. A property decorated around a personal enthusiasm reads as someone's house, and guests are less able to imagine themselves in it.

When it is executed cheaply. A half-committed theme is worse than none, because it reads as clutter rather than intent, and clutter photographs badly at thumbnail size.

The safe version

Theme one or two rooms rather than the whole property. A themed bunk room for children plus neutral, well-designed primary bedrooms serves families without excluding anyone else.

That structure captures most of the differentiation benefit at a fraction of the risk. The themed room becomes the photograph that stands out in a search grid; the rest of the property remains broadly appealing.

It is also cheaper, easier to update when the theme dates, and simpler to reverse if it does not perform.

Regional theming is the low-risk version

Leaning into the location is theming that almost never backfires, because it matches what the guest came for. A Smokies cabin that feels like a mountain cabin. A Gulf beach house that feels coastal. A Hill Country property that feels like the Hill Country.

This works because the guest chose the location, which means they already want the experience the theme references. It is theming aligned with intent rather than imposed on it.

The opposite is the most common design failure in vacation markets: a mountain cabin finished like a suburban house. It costs the same to build and it underperforms a property that committed to the setting.

Intellectual property caution

Themes referencing specific films, characters, franchises or brands carry legal exposure. Using protected characters in a commercial rental, and advertising the property using their names, is a genuine risk rather than a theoretical one.

The safe approach is theming by genre and mood rather than by property: a space-themed bunk room rather than a specific franchise, a castle theme rather than a named one.

Several platforms have also removed listings that used protected marks in titles and descriptions. If in doubt, take advice rather than assuming a small operation will not be noticed.

Keeping it current

Themes date faster than neutral design. A children's theme that was current when the property opened can read as tired within a few years, and children's tastes in particular move quickly.

Build the theme in a way that is cheap to refresh: wall decals rather than murals, bedding and soft furnishings rather than built-ins, removable rather than permanent.

That also aligns with the soft goods replacement cycle. Refreshing the theme at the same time as replacing the bedding costs almost nothing additional and keeps the property current in listing photographs.

Photograph it deliberately

A themed room is a differentiator only if it appears prominently in the listing. It should have a dedicated position in the shot order, immediately after the great room and hero amenity.

Photograph it lit warmly and staged in use. A themed bunk room made up, lit and photographed at a child's eye level reads as a feature; the same room shot flatly under overhead light reads as decoration.

Use the caption to state what it is and who it suits. Parents scanning for a property their children will enjoy are looking for exactly that signal, and the caption is where they find it.

Can I use movie characters in my rental theme?
