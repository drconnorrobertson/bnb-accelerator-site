Nashville: What a Non-Owner-Occupied Permit Is Actually Worth

Published April 3, 2026·6 min read

Two of our clients, Mahmoud and, separately, Krystin and Michael, bought four-bedroom short-term rentals in Nashville. In both cases the permit was a larger part of the analysis than the house, because in a capped market the permit is the asset.

How the cap works

Nashville distinguishes owner-occupied from non-owner-occupied short-term rental permits, and restricts non-owner-occupied permits by zoning district. In much of the residential city, a non-owner-occupied permit is simply not available.

That restriction is not a burden on the investment. It is the investment thesis. In an unregulated market, a strong year invites new supply, new supply compresses rates, and the returns that attracted capital disappear within two seasons.

A permit cap prevents exactly that. An operator holding a valid non-owner-occupied permit in a permitted zone is protected from the competition that would otherwise arrive, which is why Nashville rates have held up through periods when unregulated markets compressed.

Why the demand supports it

Nashville's distinctive demand is group travel. Bachelorette parties, corporate groups, Broadway weekends and event traffic favor a whole house within reach of downtown over separate hotel rooms, and that guest pays well and books ahead.

Four bedrooms is the entry point into that bracket. Below four, the property competes with hotel rooms on price. At four and above, it competes on the thing hotels cannot offer, which is putting the whole group under one roof.

The event calendar produces substantial compression. CMA Fest, the NFL and NHL seasons, and Music City Center conventions generate dozens of nights a year where rates run several times baseline. Capturing them requires pricing tied to the actual calendar rather than a static weekend uplift.

The permit questions to answer

  1. Does the parcel sit in a zoning district where non-owner-occupied permits are available?
  2. Does a valid permit exist on the property today, and is it current?
  3. Does the permit transfer to a new owner, or must a new application be made?
  4. If a new application is required, is one likely to be granted given current district conditions?
  5. Does any homeowner association or condominium declaration prohibit the use independently?
  6. What are the occupancy, parking and noise conditions attached to the permit?

Question three is the one that most often produces an unpleasant surprise. A property operating legally under the current owner does not guarantee that the new owner can do the same, and that distinction is worth confirming in writing with the Metro codes department.

The long-term rental floor

Every urban short-term rental should be underwritten against what it is worth as a conventional rental if the permit regime tightens. That number is the floor, and the deal has to be acceptable at the floor.

In Nashville the floor is a real one. The long-term rental market is genuinely strong, driven by population growth, a healthcare employment base and a music and hospitality economy. A property that reverted to long-term rental would not be a disaster.

That is not true of every city market. In a tourism-dependent city with weak long-term rents, a short-term rental bought purely on nightly economics is an unhedged regulatory bet. Nashville's underlying rental strength is part of what makes the permit premium rational.

Operating requirements

Urban operations are more demanding than vacation-market operations. Turnover is more frequent, response time expectations are tighter, and noise and parking complaints are far more common.

Most enforcement actions in any city begin with a neighbor complaint. Noise monitoring in shared living areas, explicit and enforced occupancy limits, clear parking assignments and a local contact reachable within thirty minutes are what keep a permitted property out of the enforcement pipeline.

In a capped market, that discipline matters more than usual, because the permit is the valuable thing and an enforcement action against it is an existential rather than financial event.

What the numbers have to clear

A Nashville four-bedroom carries a purchase price that reflects both the property and the permit, and the underwriting has to account for both.

The revenue side benefits from higher and flatter occupancy than a vacation market, plus compression nights that can run several times baseline. The expense side carries higher turnover cost, tighter guest response requirements, and management that is genuinely more demanding than a weekly beach turnover.

The stress test that matters most here is the long-term rental floor. Model what the property rents for on a twelve-month lease, subtract the same debt service, and confirm the shortfall is one you can carry. In Nashville that floor is unusually strong for a tourism-influenced city, driven by healthcare employment and sustained population growth.

If the property works at the floor, the permit premium is buying upside rather than propping up a deal that fails without it. That is the correct structure for any purchase in a capped market, and it is the discipline that separates a permitted asset from a policy bet.

Frequently asked questions

How does Nashville regulate short-term rentals?

It separates owner-occupied from non-owner-occupied permits and restricts non-owner-occupied permits by zoning district. In much of the residential city a non-owner-occupied permit is unavailable, which caps supply and protects existing operators.

Do Nashville short-term rental permits transfer on sale?

This is the critical question to confirm in writing with Metro codes before an offer. A property operating legally under the current owner does not guarantee the new owner can do the same.

Why is the long-term rental floor important in Nashville?

Because permit rules are political and can change. Nashville's long-term rental market is genuinely strong, so a property reverting to conventional rental would not be a disaster. That is not true of every tourism-dependent city.
