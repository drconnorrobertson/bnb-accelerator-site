Handling a Problem Mid-Stay Without Losing the Review

Published July 1, 2021·6 min read

A property that never has a problem during a stay does not exist. What separates properties with excellent review profiles from ones without is not the absence of problems, it is the response, and the response follows a pattern that can be learned.

Respond fast, even before you can fix it

The single largest factor in whether a mid-stay problem becomes a bad review is how quickly the guest hears from someone. Acknowledgment within minutes matters more than resolution within hours.

A guest who reports a broken air conditioner and hears nothing for two hours has already decided how they feel about the stay. The same guest who hears 'I am on it, a technician is being called now, I will update you within the hour' is in a different frame of mind.

This is why a local contact reachable within thirty minutes is worth more than a lower management fee. The response window is short.

The response sequence

  1. Acknowledge immediately and specifically. Repeat back what the problem is so the guest knows you understood.
  2. State what you are doing and when they will hear from you next.
  3. Do the thing. A technician, a replacement, a workaround.
  4. Update at the time you said you would, even if the update is that it is still in progress.
  5. Confirm resolution and check that the guest is satisfied.
  6. Offer appropriate compensation without being asked.

Step four is the one most often skipped and the one guests notice. An update that arrives when promised, even with no progress, maintains trust. Silence destroys it.

Compensation before it is requested

Offering compensation before a guest asks changes the dynamic entirely. It converts the interaction from a negotiation into a gesture, and guests respond to gestures.

Size it to the disruption. A slow wifi hour warrants an apology. A day without air conditioning in July warrants a meaningful refund. A property uninhabitable for a night warrants the night plus assistance finding alternatives.

The arithmetic is straightforward. A partial refund costs what it costs, once. A bad review suppresses bookings for months and its effect scales with how few reviews you have.

Have a workaround for the common failures

  • HVAC failure. Portable units on hand or available same day, and a relationship with a technician who answers.
  • Hot water failure. Know the water heater model and have a plumber on the bench.
  • Internet outage. A cellular hotspot as a failover, kept charged.
  • Lock failure. A documented backup entry method the guest can use.
  • Hot tub chemistry. Service on call, and a clear policy about when it is safe to use.
  • Power outage. Flashlights, charged battery packs and a note about who to call.

The point of a workaround is that it converts a stay-ending problem into an inconvenience. A guest with a portable air conditioner is uncomfortable; a guest with nothing is leaving.

When the property is genuinely uninhabitable

Occasionally a problem cannot be worked around: a burst pipe, a power outage lasting days, a storm event. At that point the priority shifts from saving the review to handling the guest well.

Refund the affected nights promptly and without argument. Help them find alternative accommodation actively rather than telling them to contact the platform. Follow up afterward.

Guests handled well in a genuinely bad situation frequently leave good reviews describing exactly that, which is worth more than a review of an uneventful stay.

The review is written before checkout

Guests form their view of a stay during it, not afterward. That means the recovery has to happen while they are still in the property, and a compensation offer made after checkout is far less effective than the same offer made on the day.

It also means the mid-stay check-in message matters. A guest who was asked how things were going and mentioned something small that got fixed remembers the fixing.

The corollary is that a problem discovered after checkout is usually unrecoverable. That is why the arrival-day check-in message exists: to surface issues while there is still time.

What not to do

Do not argue about whether the problem is real. Even where the guest is mistaken, the response is to check and explain rather than to dispute.

Do not offer compensation conditional on a review. It violates platform policy and it reads as a purchase.

Do not go silent while working on it. The gap between 'we are looking into it' and the next message is where guests decide the stay was badly handled, and it costs nothing to fill.

Frequently asked questions

How should I respond when something breaks during a guest stay?

Acknowledge immediately and specifically, state what you are doing and when they will hear from you, do it, update at the time promised even without progress, confirm resolution, and offer compensation before being asked.

Should I offer a refund before the guest asks?

Yes, sized to the disruption. Offering first converts the interaction from a negotiation into a gesture. A partial refund costs what it costs once; a bad review suppresses bookings for months.

What if the property becomes uninhabitable?

Refund affected nights promptly without argument, actively help find alternative accommodation rather than deferring to the platform, and follow up afterward. Guests handled well in a genuinely bad situation often leave good reviews describing exactly that.
