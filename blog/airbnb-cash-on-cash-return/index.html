Airbnb Cash-on-Cash Return: What Good Looks Like in 2026

Published May 31, 2026·6 min read

Cash-on-cash return is the most quoted number in short-term rental investing and the most casually manipulated. The formula is trivial. The discipline is in refusing to leave things out.

The calculation

Annual pre-tax net cash flow divided by total cash invested.

Net cash flow means gross revenue minus every operating expense minus debt service, property taxes, and insurance. Total cash invested means down payment plus closing costs plus furnishing plus any pre-launch capital work. Operating reserves sitting in your account are usually excluded, since they have not been deployed into the asset.

Worked through on a $500,000 property:

Cash-on-cash return calculation for a $500,000 short-term rental
ItemAmount
Gross annual revenue$78,000
Operating expenses (43%)-$33,500
Debt service, taxes, insurance-$40,800
Net cash flow$3,700
Cash invested (down, closing, furnishing)$146,000
Cash-on-cash return2.5%

That is a real result for a mediocre deal, and it is worth sitting with. A property producing $78,000 in gross revenue can return 2.5 percent. Gross revenue is not the deal.

What separates 2.5 percent from 16 percent

Three variables do almost all the work.

Revenue relative to price. The ratio that matters is annual gross revenue divided by purchase price. Below 13 percent, the deal is usually structurally difficult regardless of how well you operate it. Above 18 percent, you have room for mistakes. Broken Bow cabins at roughly $495,000 and $110,000 of annual revenue sit near 22 percent. Scottsdale at $1.15 million and $185,000 sits near 16 percent. Both work, for different reasons.

Expense discipline. The difference between a 15 percent co-host and a 25 percent full-service manager on $78,000 of revenue is $7,800 a year, which on $146,000 invested is 5.3 percentage points of return. That single decision moves the number more than most people expect, and it also has tax consequences for material participation.

Leverage. More leverage raises the return if the property covers its debt, and destroys it if the property does not. That is not a reason to maximize leverage, it is a reason to stress test it.

The ratio to memorize

Annual gross revenue divided by purchase price. Under 13 percent, walk. 13 to 16 percent, the deal has to be operated well. Over 18 percent, you have margin for error. It is a thirty second filter that saves weeks.

The pattern is consistent: higher entry prices compress the return even when revenue is excellent. That is a trade, not a defect. Luxury markets tend to hold value better in a downturn and attract a wealthier guest who damages less. The full picture by market is on our markets page.

Bedroom count moves the number more than almost anything else within a given market. A four-bedroom cabin and a seven-bedroom cabin in the same Sevierville corridor do not cost proportionally different amounts to buy, but the larger one commands a materially higher nightly rate and books groups that the smaller one cannot serve at all. That is why we push clients toward the largest property their capital and financing will responsibly support in cabin markets, and toward location quality rather than size in beach and urban markets where the demand driver is proximity rather than capacity.

The three ways the number gets inflated

Excluding furnishing from cash invested. If you leave $30,000 out of the denominator, a 12 percent return becomes 15 percent on identical operations. Watch for this in any pro forma someone hands you.

Using year-one revenue without a ramp discount, or year-three revenue as if it were year one. Both directions get abused depending on which way the presenter wants the number to move.

Blending in tax savings. A first-year cost segregation deduction can make the after-tax outcome extraordinary, and it is a legitimate part of the case. It is not cash-on-cash return. Report the operating number, then report the after-tax outcome separately so that deals remain comparable. Our partner firm covers the after-tax side at AE Tax Advisors.

What the metric misses

Cash-on-cash ignores principal paydown, appreciation, and the tax position, which together often exceed the cash flow itself in the early years. A property returning 12 percent in cash while amortizing $9,000 of principal and appreciating modestly is producing a total return well above the headline.

It is still the right primary filter, because it is the only component that is unambiguously real in year one. Appreciation is a forecast. Principal paydown is illiquid. Cash is cash. Use it to screen, then look at the rest, and use the full underwriting sequence before committing to anything.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate cash-on-cash return on an Airbnb?

Divide annual pre-tax net cash flow by total cash invested. Net cash flow is gross revenue minus all operating expenses minus debt service, taxes, and insurance. Total cash invested is down payment plus closing costs plus furnishing plus any capital improvements made before launch. Reserves held in the bank are usually excluded since they are not deployed.

What is a good cash-on-cash return for a short-term rental in 2026?

Twelve to eighteen percent on stabilized operations is a realistic target in the markets we underwrite. Cabin markets like Broken Bow and Sevierville run at the higher end, roughly 15 to 21 percent. Urban and luxury markets like Austin, Denver, and Scottsdale typically run 11 to 16 percent because entry prices are higher relative to revenue.

Should cash-on-cash return include tax savings?

No, not in the headline figure. Tax benefits depend on your marginal rate, your participation, and provisions that change over time, so blending them into the operating return makes deals impossible to compare. Calculate the operating cash-on-cash return first, then present the after-tax outcome separately.
