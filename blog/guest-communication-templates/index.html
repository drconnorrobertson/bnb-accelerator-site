The Five Messages Every Guest Should Get, and When

Published December 20, 2021·6 min read

Most guest problems are information problems, and most information problems are timing problems. The right information delivered at the wrong moment is the same as no information, which is why a structured sequence outperforms answering questions as they arrive.

Message one: at booking

Confirm the booking, thank them, and set expectations for what happens next and when. The specific value here is telling them when they will receive check-in details, which prevents the most common pre-arrival message.

Include anything that would cause a cancellation if discovered later: a steep driveway, stairs, a pet policy, a noise ordinance, a mandatory parking arrangement.

Disclosing friction at booking rather than at arrival is uncomfortable and it is much cheaper than the alternative. A guest who cancels at booking costs you a night you can resell. A guest who arrives and is unhappy costs you a review.

Message two: one week before

Confirm the dates, ask about arrival time, and offer anything that improves the stay: grocery delivery, a restaurant reservation, equipment rental.

This is also where local logistics belong. Where to buy groceries, how long the drive from the airport actually takes, whether a four-wheel drive vehicle is advisable in that season.

The arrival time question has operational value beyond courtesy. It tells your cleaner when the property has to be ready and flags a late arrival before it becomes a problem.

Message three: day before arrival

  • The address, with a note about anything a mapping application gets wrong.
  • The door code or key arrangement, with a photograph of the entry.
  • Parking instructions, specific and with a photograph.
  • Wifi network and password.
  • The number to call if something goes wrong, and who answers it.

Photographs matter here more than words. A picture of the front door and the parking spot eliminates most arrival confusion, particularly at night in an unfamiliar area.

Message four: shortly after check-in

A brief message asking whether they got in and whether everything is as expected. This is the highest-value message in the sequence.

It surfaces problems while they are still fixable. A guest who mentions a dead lightbulb at 4pm on arrival day gets it fixed and leaves a five star review. The same guest who says nothing writes about it a week later.

Keep it short and make clear you are not expecting a reply if all is well. Guests on vacation do not want a conversation.

Message five: the day before checkout

Checkout time, checkout instructions, and thanks. Keep the instructions minimal.

Long checkout task lists are one of the most common complaints in short-term rental reviews. Guests paid a cleaning fee and being asked to strip beds, run the dishwasher, take out the trash and vacuum reads as a double charge.

Ask for the two or three things that genuinely help the turnover and let the cleaning fee cover the rest.

The review request

A brief message after checkout asking for a review, sent once, with no pressure and nothing offered in exchange.

Guests who had a good stay frequently do not review unless prompted, and review volume matters because it dilutes the weight of any individual bad review and feeds the ranking algorithm.

Ask once. A second request reads as pressure and can produce a worse review than none at all.

Automation without sounding automated

All five messages can be automated on most management platforms, triggered by booking, days before arrival, hours after check-in and days before checkout.

The risk is that automated messages read as automated, which undercuts the trust they are meant to build. Two things fix that: writing them in a natural voice rather than a corporate one, and including specifics that could only apply to your property and market.

A message that says 'the grocery store closest to the cabin is the Food City on the Parkway, about eight minutes down the hill' reads as a person. One that says 'local amenities are available nearby' reads as a template, because it is.

Leave the post-check-in message slightly less polished than the rest. It is the one where guests are most likely to reply with a problem, and a message that reads as a genuine person asking is more likely to get an honest answer than a formatted one.

Frequently asked questions

What messages should I send Airbnb guests?

Five: booking confirmation with any friction disclosed, a one-week-out message asking arrival time, a day-before message with address, code, parking and wifi, a short post-check-in check, and a day-before-checkout message. Then a single review request.

What is the most valuable guest message?

The short check-in follow-up. It surfaces problems while they are still fixable. A guest who mentions an issue on arrival day gets it fixed and leaves a good review; the same guest who says nothing writes about it later.

Should I ask guests to do checkout tasks?

Keep it to two or three items that genuinely help the turnover. Long checkout task lists are a common review complaint, because guests who paid a cleaning fee experience it as a double charge.
