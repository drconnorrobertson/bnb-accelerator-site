Arizona's Short-Term Rental Preemption and What It Does Not Cover

Published February 24, 2022·6 min read

Arizona has the most favorable statutory position for short-term rental operators of any large state, because state law prevents cities from banning the use. Understanding precisely what that covers, and what it does not, is what makes the protection useful rather than assumed.

What the preemption does

Arizona legislation enacted in 2016 broadly preempted municipal prohibition of short-term rentals, and subsequent legislation in 2022 restored meaningful local authority to license and regulate without permitting outright bans.

The net effect is that a city in Arizona can require licensing, registration and emergency contact information, and can enforce against verified nuisance and noise violations, but cannot prohibit the use itself.

That removes the single largest risk affecting short-term rental investments elsewhere: the possibility that the property becomes unable to operate legally. For a long-hold investment, that is worth a great deal.

Short-term rental rules change frequently and the controlling rule is usually local rather than statewide. Treat this as orientation, then verify the current position with the city or county directly and read any association declaration separately. This is not legal advice.

What cities can still do

  • Require a local license or permit and charge a fee for it.
  • Require emergency contact information for a person reachable at short notice.
  • Require notification of adjacent property owners.
  • Impose and enforce noise, nuisance and occupancy standards.
  • Prohibit non-residential uses such as commercial events and weddings at a short-term rental.
  • Penalize verified violations, including suspension of a license for repeated offenses.

That is a substantial regulatory toolkit and cities use it. Scottsdale, Phoenix, Mesa, Sedona and Flagstaff all operate licensing regimes with real requirements.

What it does not reach

Homeowner association and condominium declarations. This is the exception that catches buyers most often, because the state protection is well known and the limitation is not.

A declaration is a private contract recorded against the property. State preemption of municipal authority has no effect on it, and many Arizona associations restrict or prohibit short-term rentals.

Read the full declaration, its amendments, the rules adopted under it and the recent board minutes on every Arizona purchase, exactly as you would in a state with no preemption at all.

The tax layer

A transaction privilege tax license is generally required, and state and local transaction privilege taxes apply to short-term rental income.

Platform collection covers some of this and the split varies. Confirm directly with the Arizona Department of Revenue and the municipality what remains your responsibility, and register where required even where a platform collects.

Direct bookings are generally not covered by platform collection agreements, which is a common gap for owners who build a direct channel.

What the protection is worth in practice

Compare two desert markets. In Scottsdale, the city can license and enforce but cannot ban. In Palm Springs, forty-five minutes from Joshua Tree, the city operates a permit cap with a waiting list, and a property without a permit may never obtain one.

That difference is the whole reason we transact more heavily in Arizona than in California desert markets despite California's genuinely strong demand.

It also changes the underwriting. In Arizona, regulatory risk does not require a scenario in the model. In California desert markets, the long-term rental floor is a necessary calculation.

Operating well anyway

State protection against bans does not make enforcement irrelevant. Repeated verified nuisance violations can result in license suspension, and in a market with local political friction such as Sedona, complaints are more likely to be made deliberately.

The controls are the same as anywhere: disclosed noise monitoring, enforced occupancy limits, clear parking assignments, a local contact reachable within thirty minutes, and no commercial events.

Introducing yourself to the immediate neighbors and giving them a direct number is the cheapest of these and the most effective, because it converts most potential complaints into a call you can resolve.

Where Arizona sits overall

Strong regulatory position, genuine and seasonal demand, a heated pool requirement above the entry tier, and a summer trough that has to be budgeted rather than discovered.

The East Valley submarkets differ meaningfully despite blurring together on a map. Mesa has the most inventory and the strongest spring training positioning; Gilbert and Chandler have newer stock at higher prices.

Sedona is a separate market entirely, with constrained supply, high rates relative to bedroom count, and a flatter calendar than the Phoenix metro.

Frequently asked questions

Can Arizona cities ban short-term rentals?

No. State legislation preempts municipal prohibition. Cities can require licensing, emergency contacts and neighbor notification, enforce nuisance and noise standards, and prohibit commercial events, but cannot ban the use itself.

Does Arizona preemption override HOA rules?

No, and this is the exception buyers miss most often. A declaration is a private contract recorded against the property, unaffected by preemption of municipal authority. Many Arizona associations restrict short-term rentals.

How does Arizona compare with California desert markets?

Substantially better on regulation. Arizona prevents bans; Palm Springs and San Bernardino County operate permit caps and density restrictions where a property may never obtain a permit. That difference drives our market selection.
