Airbnb Arbitrage vs Ownership: Pros, Cons, and Tax Implications

Published August 17, 2023·6 min read

Arbitrage gets promoted heavily because the entry cost is low and the story is clean: lease a unit, furnish it, keep the spread, no mortgage. That is a real business. It is also a fundamentally different business from owning, and the difference shows up most clearly on the tax return.

How each model works

Arbitrage. You sign a long-term lease, obtain written landlord consent to sublet short term, furnish the unit, and operate it. Your capital is the security deposit, first and last month, furnishing, and a small reserve. Call it $18,000 to $35,000 for a two-bedroom.

Ownership. You buy the property. Capital is the down payment, closing costs, furnishing, and reserves. For a $500,000 property that is roughly $146,000 to $167,000, as broken down in the capital requirements guide.

Roughly a five to eight times difference in capital for a comparable revenue stream. That is the entire appeal, and it is not a small one.

On that comparison arbitrage wins decisively, and any honest treatment has to say so. The cash-on-cash return is an order of magnitude better because the denominator is tiny.

What the table does not show is everything that accrues to the owner and not to the operator.

What ownership adds that the table omits

Principal paydown. Roughly $6,800 in year one on that loan, rising annually. That is return the arbitrage operator does not receive.

Appreciation. Whatever the asset does, the owner captures. The operator captures none of it.

Depreciation. This is the decisive one. An owner has depreciable basis in the building. An arbitrage operator does not, because they own no building.

That single fact eliminates cost segregation, bonus depreciation on the structure, and the entire first-year deduction that makes the Reverse Offset Method work. An arbitrage operator can depreciate furniture and equipment, which is real but small. They cannot generate a $385,000 first-year deduction, because there is no basis to accelerate.

If your primary objective is offsetting high W-2 income, arbitrage does not do it. The strategy depends on depreciable basis in real property. No basis, no deduction, regardless of how well the unit performs.

The self-employment tax difference

There is a second tax distinction that gets almost no attention.

Arbitrage is generally an active trade or business in which you provide substantial services. Net income is therefore typically subject to self-employment tax at 15.3 percent up to the wage base and 2.9 percent above it, before income tax.

An owner operating a short-term rental without substantial hotel-like services is generally reporting on Schedule E and is generally not subject to self-employment tax on the rental income, though the analysis is fact-specific and depends on the level of services provided. We go through the distinction in the self-employment tax guide, and AE Tax Advisors works through it with clients directly.

On $8,000 of arbitrage profit the difference is modest. On a portfolio of six units producing $60,000, it is roughly $9,000 a year of additional tax that an owner in the same position would not pay.

The risks are also different

Arbitrage carries lease risk. Your entire business sits on a landlord's willingness to renew, and a landlord who sees your revenue has an obvious incentive at renewal. It carries assignment risk if the building sells. And it carries regulatory risk in the sharpest form, because many ordinances specifically target non-owner-occupied subletting first.

Ownership carries market risk, liquidity risk, and a much larger capital loss if things go wrong. But you cannot be evicted from an asset you own.

There is also an asymmetry at the end of the road. When an arbitrage operator stops operating, they have furniture and a customer list. When an owner stops operating, they have a property they can sell, refinance, convert to a long-term rental, exchange into something else, or simply hold. The arbitrage business has revenue but very little terminal value, which is why operators who do well at it almost always use the cash flow to buy something eventually.

Deciding between the two?

If the objective is tax offset, ownership is the only path. If the objective is fast cash flow with limited capital, we will tell you so plainly.

Who each model is for

Arbitrage suits an operator with limited capital, time to run the business, and a cash flow objective. It is a job with good returns on invested capital and no terminal asset.

Ownership suits a high earner whose primary problem is a tax bill and whose secondary objective is a long-term asset. That is the profile we work with, which is why every property in the markets we underwrite is an acquisition rather than a lease.

Frequently asked questions

What is Airbnb arbitrage?

Airbnb arbitrage is leasing a property long term from a landlord, obtaining written permission to sublet it on a short-term basis, furnishing it, and keeping the spread between short-term revenue and the lease payment plus operating costs. The operator never owns the real estate.

Is Airbnb arbitrage still profitable in 2026?

It can be, but margins have compressed as more operators entered and landlords became aware of the spread. Typical net margins run 10 to 20 percent of gross revenue after rent, cleaning, utilities, and platform fees. The model remains capital-light, which is its main advantage.

Does Airbnb arbitrage qualify for the short-term rental tax loophole?

Not in the way ownership does. An arbitrage operator has no depreciable basis in the building, so there is no cost segregation and no large first-year depreciation deduction. Furniture and equipment can be depreciated, and the activity is generally an active trade or business, which means net income is typically subject to self-employment tax.
