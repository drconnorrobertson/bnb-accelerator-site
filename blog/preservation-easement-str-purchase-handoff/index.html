Buying an STR with an existing preservation agreement

Published October 7, 2026·7 min read

A seller can deliver a renovated historic home without delivering a complete record of what its preservation agreement requires. Before buying it as an STR, establish whether the proposed handoff includes the controlling documents, protected-condition records, necessary approvals and a workable plan for ongoing obligations. The existing renovation guide asks whether planned work can be approved. This guide asks what a buyer is taking over when an agreement is already recorded and the seller says the work is finished. Substantial cash or a large tax bill does not answer that question. Discuss the purchase and opening plan using a non-sensitive status memo while qualified counsel and the identified agreement holder review the property-specific file.

Identify the actual preservation agreement, not just the designation

The National Park Service Historic Preservation Fund guidance describes grant-related recorded preservation instruments and continuing responsibilities. Its sample includes condition documentation, maintenance, access, inspection and successor provisions. NPS says the model needs adaptation to applicable law. These are examples of questions to investigate, not terms proven to bind your purchase. The page was last updated November 9, 2023 and checked October 7, 2026.

Request the actual recorded instrument and amendments, referenced exhibits, applicable grant-related documents and holder contact through authorized channels. Ask local counsel to distinguish the grant recipient, current owner and easement holder. Determine which provisions bind this buyer and which consent, notice or transfer steps apply. A seller-provided generic form is not the executed agreement.

Use the historic renovation guide for designation, building review and future alteration planning. Use the title guide for conveyed interests and proposed coverage. Neither a National Register label nor an STR permit establishes compliance with a separate recorded agreement. Have counsel resolve conflicts before the contract requires an irreversible choice.

Compare the protected-condition record with what you are buying

Build a three-column comparison, with qualified help: documented protected condition, present condition and the evidence resolving each difference. Match photographs or drawings to named rooms and features rather than collecting an undated image folder. Ask the seller to identify completed changes, who performed them and the approval or closeout file relied on. A contractor invoice shows a charge; it does not alone establish the holder’s acceptance.

Translate actual access requirements into an operating calendar

Ask the holder and counsel which access, notice, scheduling and use provisions apply. Do not import a day count from the NPS sample into every property. Find out whether any proposed dates or arrangements have been accepted and how they interact with guest privacy, safety, staffing and insurance. A manager’s willingness to unlock a door is not the same as an approved access plan.

Original hypothetical calendar test: assume an independently reviewed agreement and accepted schedule require six daytime access sessions for this example. The buyer’s first draft places all six during booked stays. A revised plan places four during already-blocked setup periods; two still conflict with offered dates. The unresolved count is two sessions, not automatically two lost rental nights. Actual notice, opening hours, permitted areas and guest turnover determine the effect.

Do not multiply sessions by nightly rate and call the result lost revenue. Determine whether a compliant arrangement changes available nights, then examine the demand for those particular dates and distinct event or staffing costs. Do not count the same setup block again as access-related downtime. If the operating plan needs a change to the agreement, treat it as unapproved until the necessary parties and procedures have resolved it.

Confirm who maintains the calendar, communicates with the holder and records completion. Put the accepted responsibility in the manager’s scope; identify owner work that is excluded. This is acquisition feasibility, not generic hosting advice. The purchase-to-launch guide keeps these dependencies separate from the first guest booking.

Reconcile corrective work and annual responsibilities without double counting

A normal condition report and a preservation-related response may describe the same repair differently. Ask the professionals to reconcile the scope before adding both quotes. Treat an unresolved requirement as unresolved rather than inventing a restoration bill. Determine actual payer allocation and closing treatment with counsel, the lender and settlement team; do not assume the seller owes every past or future item.

Hypothetical quote comparison only: the acquisition model includes $18,000 for roof work. A qualified contractor then quotes $26,000 for the same defined scope using the professionally confirmed acceptable specification. Replace the $18,000 input with $26,000: the incremental requirement is $8,000, not another $26,000 or a combined $44,000. If one quote includes scaffolding or another includes different areas, first make the scopes comparable.

Separately assume six accepted access sessions need $175 of additional staffing each, and a distinct annual reporting service costs $650. The incremental annual allocation is $1,050 + $650 = $1,700. If management already includes either service, remove the duplicate addition. These figures are invented, not market prices or NPS fees. Recurring services are separate from the $8,000 one-time work increase, and neither calculation values the property or predicts bookings.

Keep restoration after a casualty as a separate adviser and insurance question, not a priced assumption from an ordinary maintenance quote. Ask what the actual policy, agreement and approval process cover. Do not assume insurance proceeds, a grant reimbursement or a tax credit will fund the purchase. Make the handoff a closing condition, not a promise to explain later

Before committing, produce a short closing memo: identified agreement and holder, baseline/current-condition comparison, written status of decisive differences, accepted access plan, complete cost reconciliation and owner/manager responsibility split. Ask counsel what protection the actual contract provides while answers are pending. Neither a document request nor this checklist creates a right to extend, cancel or recover a deposit.

Proceed when the reviewed obligations fit the lawful STR use, viable operating plan, acceptable financing and funded budget. Renegotiate a confirmed cost or responsibility through valid terms. Delay under adequate contract protection if a decisive record or response is missing. Decline when the acquisition depends on unsupported compliance, incompatible access promises or unpriced work beyond your risk limit.

Frequently asked questions

Is a preservation agreement the same as historic-district approval?

No. Review the actual agreement separately from public designation, building approval and rental permission. The relevant documents and authorities must answer for this purchase.

Does the NPS sample determine my property’s access schedule?

No. It is a model, not your executed instrument. Have counsel and the identified holder establish the applicable terms and a workable accepted plan.

Can I assume completed seller repairs satisfy preservation obligations?

No. Compare current condition with the referenced records and obtain relevant approvals or written status. A receipt alone does not resolve missing compliance evidence.

Should I add the entire revised repair quote to my old budget?

Not if it replaces the same scope. Reconcile the quotes, replace the old input and count only distinct additional work or services separately.
