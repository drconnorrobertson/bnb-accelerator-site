Acquisition Diligence

What if the STR you want to buy has leased solar panels?

Published September 24, 2026·6 min read

A solar array on a short-term rental may belong to the seller, a lender, or a third-party solar company. Those are different transactions. Before pricing the home or counting utility savings, obtain the signed solar contract, determine who owns the panels, ask whether the buyer must qualify to assume the contract, and show every future payment and roof obligation in the underwriting. Solar production does not make a lease payment disappear, and an array on the roof is not automatically part of the real estate being sold.

Answer the ownership question first

Ask for the purchase receipt or loan agreement, lease or power-purchase agreement (PPA), all amendments, current balance or buyout schedule, system warranty, installation permit, utility interconnection approval, production records, roof warranty, insurance documents, and any UCC or fixture filing. Have title and the proposed lender reconcile the contract with the seller's disclosures and land records. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's solar-financing overview distinguishes solar loans, leases, and PPAs; a house sale can require payoff or assumption depending on the agreement.

Buying an STR where solar is part of the selling pitch? Ask BNB Accelerator to compare the utility benefit, transfer obligation, loan terms, and roof exposure in the acquisition model. The buyer's lender, title company, insurer, contractor, and counsel should review the actual documents.

The general solar buying guide covers equipment as an amenity. This page addresses whether the buyer can take over a third-party contract and what that does to cash needed at closing and monthly return.

Separate four contract structures

StructureWhat the buyer needsPotential model error
Seller owns panels free and clearProof of ownership, condition, output, warranty transfer, roof reviewAssigning savings without checking production or utility tariff
Seller has separate solar loanPayoff, security documents, lien or UCC treatment, closing instructionsAdding panel value while leaving the debt unresolved
Solar leaseTerm, payment and escalator, assignment approval, buyout, maintenance and roof clausesIgnoring a fixed payment that continues during low occupancy
PPAPrice per kWh, escalator, minimum purchase if any, production records, transfer approvalTreating generated power as free electricity

The contract label may not reveal every obligation. Read the schedule of payments, maintenance exclusions, monitoring subscription, inverter replacement, roof removal and reinstallation, end-of-term options, and default clauses. Ask the provider in writing what it needs to approve a buyer, how long approval takes, and whether the seller can pay off or buy out the agreement if assumption fails. Put the answer before the closing date, not after it.

Let the proposed lender classify the panels

The current Fannie Mae Selling Guide treats owned, separately financed, leased, and PPA panels differently. For a lease or PPA, it requires the lender to review the agreement; it says third-party-owned panels cannot be included in the property's appraised value. Payment treatment depends on the contract. It also addresses roof-damage responsibility, alternate power, and title exceptions. This is a useful example of lender diligence, not a claim that every STR loan follows Fannie Mae rules.

Send the full contract to the actual lender before the financing contingency ends. Ask whether the obligation changes debt-to-income or debt-service calculations, loan-to-value, appraisal, title, insurance, and cash to close. A seller may advertise a $30,000 installation, but a buyer cannot automatically add that historical spend to real-estate value when a third party owns the equipment. If a PACE assessment or solar debt appears in land records, get a specific title and lender closing condition rather than assuming a credit solves it.

Normalize utility cost using bills and output

Obtain at least twelve consecutive months of utility bills and solar production reports, ideally including seasonal peaks. Reconcile grid purchases, exports and credits, the solar-company bill, fees, and any true-up or minimum payment. The seller may quote a small grid bill while omitting the separate solar bill. A property with guests, a pool heater, hot tub, EV charger, or heavy summer air-conditioning can also use a different load from the seller's own occupancy pattern.

Illustrative only: the seller shows an average $80 monthly grid bill and a $190 monthly solar lease with a 2.9% annual escalator. The current combined cash expense is $270 per month, not $80. At a constant grid bill, the lease alone would be about $252 per month in year ten, bringing the combined illustrative cash expense to about $332. Actual bills, tariff changes, system output, and usage will differ. The point is to model both payees and the contractual escalator instead of capitalizing a marketing claim about savings.

Use the utility normalization guide to separate owner-use history from the proposed guest load. For the acquisition case, test a low-production year and the cost of a repair or roof project even if a warranty exists.

Inspect the roof and the exit path

A roof near replacement age can turn solar into a closing issue. Ask a roofer and qualified solar contractor what removal and reinstallation would cost, who may perform it under the warranty, and which party pays under the lease or PPA. Inspect penetrations and wiring; do not rely on a general home inspection to determine solar-system performance. The U.S. Department of Energy's buyer guide recommends investigating the system's age, maintenance history, and terms of purchase.

Also test a resale before the solar contract ends. Will a later buyer need to qualify, pay a fee, or accept rising payments? Can the seller buy out the contract, and at what formula? If the solar company can remove panels, what happens to the roof afterward? The answers affect both current price and exit liquidity. Record the provider's transfer procedure and the buyer's lender response in the permanent property file.

Decision rule before closing

Proceed when ownership and security interests are clear, provider transfer approval is available on known terms, the lender and title company accept the documents, roof and electrical condition are documented, and the full utility-plus-solar cost fits the conservative STR model.

Renegotiate when the seller can pay off a balance, fund a documented roof-removal cost, or obtain a transfer approval before closing. Put the payoff, lien release, assumption, or buyout condition in writing with counsel. Do not accept an open-ended seller promise that the solar company will approve the buyer later.

Extend or decline when the contract is unavailable, provider approval is uncertain, payments or buyout cannot be quantified, a senior lien cannot be resolved, or the property depends on savings that the bills do not support. Frequently asked questions

Do leased solar panels transfer automatically with an STR sale?

Do not assume so. Review the signed lease or PPA and obtain the provider's written transfer requirements, timing, fees, and approval before closing.

Can an appraiser include leased panels in the home's value?

Fannie Mae's current guide says third-party-owned leased or PPA panels cannot be included in appraised property value for its covered loans. The actual lender and appraiser must evaluate the transaction's governing rules.

Does a low utility bill prove solar saves money?

No. Add the separate solar payment or PPA charges, fees, utility true-ups, and contractual escalators, then compare production and usage across a full year.

Who pays to remove panels for a roof replacement?

The answer depends on the signed agreement and warranties. Get written contractor pricing and the provider's process before accepting the seller's estimate.
