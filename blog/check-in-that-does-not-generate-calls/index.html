Designing a Check-In That Does Not Generate Phone Calls

Published September 1, 2025·6 min read

Nearly every guest problem in the first hour is an information problem, and nearly every information problem is a timing problem. A check-in designed properly eliminates most of them before the guest leaves home.

What guests actually need, and when

The day before arrival, not the morning of: the address with a note about anything a mapping application gets wrong, the door code or key arrangement, parking instructions, and the wifi network and password.

Photographs matter more than words. A picture of the front door and the parking space eliminates most arrival confusion, particularly at night in an unfamiliar area.

Send it as a single message rather than across several. A guest hunting through a message thread at 10pm for a door code is a phone call waiting to happen.

The mapping problem

In rural and mountain markets, mapping applications routinely send guests to the wrong place. Gravel drives, unnamed private roads and addresses that resolve to a nearby parcel are common.

The fix is to state it explicitly: 'GPS will take you past the driveway. Look for the green mailbox on the right, about 200 yards after the fork.' That sentence prevents more calls than any other single piece of information.

Where the problem is severe, provide coordinates alongside the address and mention it in the listing so guests are not surprised.

Hardware that works

  • Smart lock with a unique code per booking, which is both convenient and a security improvement over a shared code.
  • Batteries on a replacement schedule, because a dead battery is a locked-out guest at 11pm.
  • A backup entry method and a documented process for using it.
  • Lighting at the entry and along the path, since most arrivals in vacation markets are after dark.
  • Clear house numbering visible from the road at night.

Lockboxes work and are less good than smart locks, because codes get shared and cannot be changed between bookings without someone visiting the property.

The first five minutes inside

Guests arriving after a long drive want three things: lights on, comfortable temperature, and to know where the bathroom is. Everything else can wait.

Smart thermostats set to a comfortable temperature before arrival are a genuine improvement over a house that is 55 degrees in February or 90 in August. It is a small operational touch that appears in reviews.

A single printed card near the entry with wifi, checkout time and the contact number covers the questions guests ask most, and it works when a phone battery does not.

The house guide, kept short

A long house manual does not get read. A short one does, and the difference is what goes in it.

Include only what a guest needs in the first hour and what prevents problems: how the heat works, how the hot tub works, where the trash goes, quiet hours, and anything unusual about the property.

Local recommendations belong in a separate section or a separate document. They are genuinely appreciated and they are not what the arriving guest is looking for at 10pm.

The post-arrival message

A short message an hour or two after check-in asking whether they got in and whether everything is as expected. This is the highest-value message in the entire guest sequence.

It surfaces problems while they are still fixable. A guest who mentions a dead lightbulb on arrival day gets it fixed and leaves a five star review; the same guest who says nothing writes about it a week later.

Keep it brief and make clear no reply is needed if all is well. Guests on vacation do not want a conversation, they want to know someone is reachable.

What breaks a good check-in

Unclear parking is the most common failure after mapping. Guests arriving in two vehicles who cannot work out where to put them start the stay annoyed, and it is entirely preventable with a photograph and a sentence.

The second is a code that does not work, which is usually a battery or a code that was not updated between bookings. Both are solved by a schedule rather than by attention.

The third is nobody answering the phone. A local contact reachable within thirty minutes is worth more than a lower management percentage, and it is the single thing that converts a bad arrival into a recoverable one.

Frequently asked questions

What should I send guests before arrival?

The day before, in a single message: the address with any mapping caveat, the door code, parking instructions with a photograph, and the wifi details. Photographs of the door and parking space prevent most arrival confusion.

Should I use a smart lock or a lockbox?

A smart lock with a unique code per booking, on a battery replacement schedule with a documented backup entry method. Lockbox codes get shared and cannot be changed between bookings without visiting the property.

What is the most valuable guest message?

A short check-in follow-up an hour or two after arrival. It surfaces problems while they are still fixable, which is the difference between a fixed lightbulb and a review mentioning it.
