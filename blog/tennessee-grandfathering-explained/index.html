Tennessee's Grandfathering Protection and What It Actually Covers

Published June 2, 2022·6 min read

Tennessee sits in an unusual middle position. It has no general preemption preventing local governments from regulating short-term rentals, but it does have a state act that protects units already operating when a local ordinance takes effect, which is a meaningful and specific protection.

What the act does

The Tennessee Short Term Rental Unit Act, enacted in 2018, generally prevents local governments from prohibiting short-term rental units that were lawfully operating before the local ordinance took effect.

That is grandfathering at the state level, and it is genuinely valuable. In markets where restriction arrives, being already lawfully operating is the difference between a protected asset and a stranded one.

It also creates a specific incentive: in a Tennessee market where regulation looks likely, entering and operating lawfully before the ordinance passes puts a property inside the protection.

Short-term rental rules change frequently and the controlling rule is usually local rather than statewide. Treat this as orientation, then verify the current position with the city or county directly and read any association declaration separately. This is not legal advice.

What it does not do

It does not prevent local governments from regulating new units. Cities retain broad authority over permitting, zoning, occupancy limits, noise standards and inspection requirements for units that were not already operating.

The word 'lawfully' is doing real work. A unit operating without required permits or in violation of existing rules is not lawfully operating and is generally outside the protection.

It also does not reach homeowner association declarations, which bind independently as they do everywhere.

Nashville's structure

Nashville distinguishes owner-occupied from non-owner-occupied permits and restricts non-owner-occupied permits by zoning district. In much of the residential city, a non-owner-occupied permit is unavailable.

That restriction is the investment thesis rather than an obstacle. A permit cap prevents the supply growth that compresses rates in unregulated markets, which is why permitted Nashville properties hold their position.

The critical diligence question is transferability. A property operating legally under the current owner does not guarantee the new owner can do the same, and that has to be confirmed in writing with Metro codes before an offer.

The Smokies corridor

Sevier County and the Smokies cities, Gatlinburg, Pigeon Forge and Sevierville, have long-established cabin rental economies with correspondingly established regulatory frameworks.

This is the structurally most stable kind of market: rental accommodation predates the regulatory debate and underpins the local economy, which makes restrictive change politically difficult in a way it is not in a residential city.

It is our most-transacted market for a combination of reasons, and regulatory stability is one of them alongside the demand base and the depth of inventory.

The tax picture

Tennessee levies no state income tax on wages. That does not change the federal treatment of a short-term rental, and it does remove a layer of state-level complexity that a comparable California or New York purchase would carry.

State and local occupancy and sales taxes apply to short-term rental revenue. Platforms collect some of these automatically and not others depending on the jurisdiction.

Confirm with the state and the local authority what remains your responsibility, and register where required even where a platform collects, since registration obligations frequently apply regardless.

Verifying a Tennessee purchase

  1. Establish the governing jurisdiction from the parcel, since county and city rules differ.
  2. Confirm whether short-term rental is permitted in that zone and what permit is required.
  3. Confirm whether an existing permit transfers on sale.
  4. Establish whether the property was lawfully operating and when, for grandfathering purposes.
  5. Read the full association declaration where one exists.
  6. Confirm occupancy and sales tax registration obligations.

Where Tennessee sits overall

Favorable, with strong local variation. The state act provides a meaningful protection for existing operators, the Smokies corridor is structurally stable, and Nashville's cap protects permitted operators from supply growth.

The variation is real. Chattanooga and Memphis operate their own regimes with zoning and density requirements, and the answer in one Tennessee market tells you nothing about another.

For an investor, the practical guidance is that Tennessee rewards doing the parcel-level verification properly, because the state-level headline is favorable and the local detail is where the answer actually lives.

How this affects timing

Grandfathering creates a specific and unusual incentive: in a Tennessee market where regulation looks likely, entering and operating lawfully before an ordinance passes puts the property inside the protection.

That is a genuine consideration in growing markets where local debate has started but no ordinance exists yet. The property that is lawfully operating when the rule arrives is in a different position from the one that closes three months later.

It is not a reason to rush a purchase. A property bought at a poor basis to capture a grandfathering position is still a poor purchase, and the protection does not fix the economics.

It is a reason, where two comparable properties are under consideration and one is in a market with an active regulatory debate, to weigh the timing rather than treating it as neutral. And it is a reason to complete permitting and registration promptly after closing rather than treating it as an administrative task to get to eventually.

What does Tennessee's short-term rental act protect?

It generally prevents local governments from prohibiting units that were lawfully operating before a local ordinance took effect. The word lawfully matters: a unit operating without required permits is generally outside the protection.

Can Nashville still restrict short-term rentals?

Yes, for units not already lawfully operating. Nashville restricts non-owner-occupied permits by zoning district, and in much of the residential city such a permit is unavailable.

Does Tennessee have state income tax on rental income?

Tennessee levies no state income tax on wages, which removes a layer of state-level complexity, though it does not change the federal treatment of the property. Occupancy and sales taxes still apply to rental revenue.
