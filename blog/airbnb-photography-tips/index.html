Airbnb Photography Tips: How to Get More Bookings

Published November 9, 2023·4 min read

Every guest decision starts the same way: a grid of thumbnails, a price, and a rating. Your listing gets somewhere between one and two seconds to earn a click. Everything else you have built, the furnishing, the amenities, the pricing strategy, the reviews, only matters if that first image wins the click.

This is the highest-return few hundred dollars in the entire launch, and it is routinely the line item owners cut.

Hire a professional, and hire the right one

Phone photos cost you money every single day the listing is live. Modern phone cameras are genuinely good, and they still lose to a professional with a wide lens, off-camera lighting, and experience with interiors, because interior photography is a lighting problem before it is a camera problem.

Hire someone who shoots short-term rentals specifically, not general real estate. Real estate photography sells a building to a buyer: wide, empty, clinical. Short-term rental photography sells an experience to a guest: warm, occupied-looking, detail-rich. The two look different and convert differently.

Expect $300 to $900 for a standard property and up to $2,000 for a large luxury property with drone, twilight, and lifestyle work. Ask to see a full gallery from a comparable property, not a highlight reel.

Prepare before the shoot

The photographer is only as good as the room. Two hours of prep changes the output more than a better camera would.

  • Every bed fully made with pressed white linens and a styled throw. Iron the linens. It shows.
  • All surfaces cleared, then restaged intentionally. A coffee setup, a stack of books, a bowl of citrus.
  • Every light in the house on, including lamps and under-cabinet lighting, and every bulb matched in color temperature. Mixed warm and cool bulbs in one frame is the fastest way to make a nice room look cheap.
  • Blinds and curtains open, windows cleaned.
  • Bathrooms with fresh folded towels, no personal items, no visible plumbing hardware clutter.
  • Outdoors staged and functional: hot tub filled and clean, cushions on, fire pit laid, grill lid closed, string lights on for twilight shots.
  • Cords hidden, remotes stored, trash cans out of frame, cars moved off the driveway.

The shot list

Insist on this coverage:

  1. Hero exterior, ideally with the amenity or view visible, shot in soft light.
  2. Twilight exterior with interior lights on. This is the single most compelling image most properties have.
  3. Great room from two angles.
  4. Kitchen wide, plus one detail shot of the coffee station or island.
  5. Dining space showing the full seating count.
  6. Every bedroom, one wide each, plus a detail on the primary.
  7. Every bathroom, including the ones you are not proud of. Omission reads as concealment.
  8. The amenity: hot tub, pool, game room, theater, bunk room, whatever the market filters for.
  9. Outdoor living, day and evening.
  10. The view, if there is one, shot honestly.
  11. Drone for properties where setting matters: lakefront, mountain, beach proximity.
  12. Workspace, if you want to appear in remote-work filters.

Ordering, which most owners get wrong

Photo order is a merchandising decision, not a walkthrough.

Image one is your thumbnail and it competes at postage-stamp size in a grid. It has to be legible and distinct when small. A gorgeous but busy wide interior loses to a clean image of a lit hot tub deck at that scale.

Images two through five should carry the reasons to book: the amenity, the great room, the view, the standout space. Guests who swipe past five images are already interested; guests who do not are gone.

Then bedrooms, bathrooms, and utility spaces in a logical order.

Caption every image. Captions are read more than descriptions and they carry information the photo cannot: bed sizes, which floor a room is on, whether the hot tub is private, how far the beach is. They also give you a place to answer the question that would otherwise become a pre-booking message.

Honesty is an operations decision

Photos that oversell produce a booking and a four-star review, and review average is worth more than a single booking. Do not shoot the one angle that hides the neighbor's shed. Do not use a lens so wide the living room reads twice its size. Do not photograph a view from a spot no guest can stand.

The goal is a guest who walks in and finds the property matches or exceeds the listing. That guest leaves five stars, and five stars compounds.

When to reshoot

Reshoot after any material change: new furniture, a new amenity, a renovation, a new deck. Reshoot seasonally if your market has a strong second season, because a Poconos property that only shows summer lake photos loses ski season bookings, and a Smokies cabin without fall foliage images is giving away its best month.

Also reshoot if your click-through rate is weak. If your listing gets impressions but few clicks, that is a thumbnail problem, not a pricing problem. Swap image one and watch what happens over the next two weeks before you touch the pricing.

Photography is not decoration. It is the top of the funnel, and everything downstream is multiplied by it.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Airbnb photography cost?

Professional short-term rental photography commonly runs $300 to $900 for a standard property and $900 to $2,000 for a large luxury property with drone work, twilight exteriors, and lifestyle staging. Against a property grossing six figures a year, that is one of the highest-return line items in the entire launch budget, and it is the last place to economize.

How many photos should an Airbnb listing have?

Roughly 30 to 40 well-chosen images for most properties, and up to 60 for large multi-room cabins. Beyond that, guests stop scrolling and the marginal image adds nothing. What matters more than count is coverage: every bedroom, every bathroom, every shared space, the amenity, the exterior, and the view. A missing room reads to guests as a room you are hiding.

What should the first Airbnb photo be?

The image that best communicates why someone would book this specific property, which is usually the hero amenity or the most distinctive space rather than a generic exterior. In cabin markets that is often the hot tub deck with a view. In Scottsdale it is the pool. The thumbnail is competing in a grid of thumbnails, so it must be legible and distinct at small size, not merely attractive at full size.
