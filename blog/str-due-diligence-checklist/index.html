Short-Term Rental Due Diligence Checklist

Published May 10, 2026·6 min read

Standard residential inspection covers a house someone will live in. A short-term rental is a small commercial hospitality operation in a residential shell, and several of the things most likely to cost you money are not on a standard inspection checklist.

Documents, before anything physical

  1. Municipal rules and permit status for the specific address, in writing, including whether a permit transfers on sale. See the verification checklist.
  2. Full HOA or association package, including covenants, bylaws, rules, recent amendments, meeting minutes, and assessment history.
  3. Trailing twelve month platform exports if the property operates, showing every booking with dates, plus the expense ledger. See how to use this in negotiation.
  4. An insurance quote for the actual use, not an estimate. In coastal and wildfire exposed markets this single item decides deals. See the insurance guide.
  5. Utility history, which reveals the real cost of heating a property that runs year round with heavy guest use.

Physical items that matter more in a rental

  • Water heater capacity relative to occupancy. A property sleeping fourteen with a residential water heater will generate reviews about cold showers within the first month.
  • HVAC sizing and zoning, since guests set thermostats aggressively and a marginal system fails under that load.
  • Septic capacity and condition, which is the single most expensive surprise in rural cabin markets and is sized for a family rather than a rotating group of twelve.
  • Well output and water quality where applicable, tested rather than assumed.
  • Decks, railings, and stairs, which carry both liability and code exposure under heavy use.
  • Hot tub and pool equipment age, including whether the electrical is compliant.
  • Road access and grade, particularly in mountain markets, where a steep unpaved approach costs winter bookings and generates complaints year round.
  • Internet service availability and actual speed, tested on site rather than taken from a provider coverage map.

Safety items that are frequently licensing requirements

  • Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in required locations.
  • Egress from sleeping areas, especially in finished basements marketed as bedrooms.
  • Handrails and guardrails meeting code.
  • Pool and hot tub barriers or covers where required.
  • Fire extinguisher placement.

A basement bedroom without conforming egress is a common issue, and it matters twice: it may not count as a bedroom for licensing, and it affects the sleeping capacity your revenue model assumed.

Tie the findings back to the model

Every diligence finding is either a price adjustment, a budget line, or a reason to walk. A septic system at the end of its life is a capital item to fund, not a surprise to absorb. An undersized water heater is a modest fix. An unpermitted addition counted as two bedrooms in the listing is a revenue model problem, not a repair.

The purpose of diligence is not to produce a list. It is to update the model. See how to analyze an Airbnb deal.

Frequently asked questions

What should I inspect when buying a short-term rental?

Beyond a standard inspection: water heater capacity relative to maximum occupancy, HVAC sizing under aggressive guest use, septic capacity and condition, well output and quality, decks and railings, hot tub and pool equipment age, road access and grade, and on site internet speed.

What documents should I verify before buying a short-term rental?

Municipal rules and permit status for the specific address including transferability, the full HOA package with amendments and assessment history, trailing twelve month platform exports and expense ledger if operating, an insurance quote for the actual use, and utility history.

Why does septic matter so much in cabin markets?

Because systems are typically sized for a family rather than a rotating group of ten or twelve, and failure is the single most expensive surprise in rural markets. Capacity and condition should be inspected specifically against the occupancy your revenue model assumes.

What if a bedroom does not have conforming egress?

It matters twice. It may not count as a bedroom for licensing purposes, and it affects the sleeping capacity your revenue model assumed, which makes it a revenue problem rather than a repair item. Finished basement bedrooms are the most common instance.
