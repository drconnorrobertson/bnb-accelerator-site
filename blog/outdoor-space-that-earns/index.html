Designing Outdoor Space That Actually Earns Its Cost

Published December 11, 2025·6 min read

In almost every vacation rental market, the outdoor space is the product. It is where guests spend the evening, what the hero photograph shows, and what the review mentions. It is also where owners most often spend money on the wrong things.

The hierarchy

First, the hero amenity: hot tub in mountain and ski markets, heated pool in desert and Florida markets, dock and water access at the lake. This is what the guest filtered for.

Second, covered outdoor living with seating for the full occupancy. Weather happens everywhere, and covered space is what keeps guests outside rather than moving indoors.

Third, a fire feature. Fire pits get used most of the year in most markets and are among the cheapest additions with a genuine effect on booking and review language.

Everything else is optional, and money spent on it before these three is misallocated.

Seating for the actual occupancy

A ten-person house with four outdoor chairs has failed the primary use case. Group properties gather outside, and the space has to hold everyone at once.

This is one of the cheapest gaps to fix and one of the most commonly present. Outdoor furniture for ten costs a fraction of what a hot tub does and is mentioned in reviews with similar frequency.

Photograph it at full capacity. A deck photographed with every seat filled and the table set communicates capacity faster than any description.

Lighting is underrated

Outdoor lighting extends the usable hours of the space and transforms the photography. A twilight exterior shot with warm lighting is the single most effective differentiator in mountain, lake and beach markets.

String lights, path lighting and warm fixtures cost very little and change how the property reads in a thumbnail grid, which is where the booking decision starts.

It also has a practical function. Guests arriving after dark in an unfamiliar property navigate steps, hot tubs and paths, and poor lighting is both a review problem and an injury risk.

What to skip

  • Elaborate outdoor kitchens. A quality grill plus a prep surface covers what guests actually do. Full outdoor kitchens are expensive and rarely used to capacity.
  • Trampolines and recreational equipment with injury exposure. Several insurers exclude them and the booking benefit is minimal.
  • Delicate landscaping. It requires maintenance and guests will not notice it. Low-maintenance planting reads the same in photographs.
  • Outdoor televisions. Expensive, fail in weather, and guests came outside to be outside.
  • Water features other than the hero amenity. Fountains and ponds add maintenance without booking effect.

The maintenance reality

Outdoor space degrades faster than interiors. Sun, rain, salt, freeze and guest use all take a toll, and outdoor furniture that looks fine in year one frequently looks tired by year three.

Budget a shorter replacement cycle for outdoor soft goods than for interior ones, and choose materials accordingly. Powder-coated aluminum and quality resin wicker survive; cheap steel and untreated wood do not.

Hot tubs and pools require scheduled service rather than reactive attention. Hot tub chemistry problems produce health complaints and bad reviews, and weekly service is not optional.

The privacy question

In markets where the guest is buying seclusion, such as Hocking Hills, Blue Ridge and much of the Smokies, screening matters as much as the amenity. A hot tub with a neighbor visible from it is worth measurably less than one without.

Tree screening, fencing and orientation are the tools, and orientation is free. Positioning outdoor space to face the view and away from the neighbor is a design decision rather than a spending one.

In dense urban and resort markets the calculation reverses. Guests expect proximity, and the requirement shifts from privacy to noise control, which is a management problem rather than a design one.

Sequencing the spend

Where the budget cannot cover everything at once, the order is hero amenity, then seating for full occupancy, then lighting, then fire feature, then everything else. That sequence tracks how much each item affects the booking decision.

The common error is reversing it: spending on landscaping and decorative elements while the deck seats four in a ten-person house. The landscaping does not appear in any guest's decision; the seating does.

It is also worth funding the hero amenity at purchase rather than deferring it. A cabin without a hot tub in the Smokies competes against cabins with one from day one, and the underperformance starts immediately rather than waiting for the upgrade.

Frequently asked questions

What outdoor features matter most in a short-term rental?

The market's hero amenity first, meaning hot tub in mountain markets and heated pool in desert and Florida markets, then covered outdoor living with seating for the full occupancy, then a fire feature. Everything else is optional.

Is an outdoor kitchen worth it?

Usually not. A quality grill and a prep surface cover what guests actually do. Full outdoor kitchens are expensive and rarely used to capacity, and the same money spent on seating and lighting produces more effect.

How does outdoor lighting affect bookings?

Substantially, through photography. A twilight exterior with warm lighting is the most effective differentiator in mountain, lake and beach markets, and it costs very little relative to its effect on the thumbnail.
