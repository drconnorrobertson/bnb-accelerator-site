Architect vs Interior Designer for a Rental Renovation

AI-assisted editorial guide · Published November 20, 2026

An architect and an interior designer may both contribute to a rental renovation, but a job title alone does not establish the work included or legally permitted. Some projects need coordinated building design; others focus on interior layouts, finishes and furnishings. Define the proposed changes, then verify the qualifications and jurisdictional requirements for the actual documents and services needed.

Describe the project before choosing a role

Separate furniture replacement from changes to walls, building systems, circulation or use. List known constraints and the questions that remain unresolved. A mood board cannot answer every technical issue, while a construction drawing set may not include purchasing every furnishing. Ask potential providers to identify what they will produce and what requires another specialist.

Check the applicable practice rules

Interior design regulation varies between jurisdictions and can protect titles, regulate specific practice or combine elements of both. CIDQ explains those distinctions. Verify local requirements directly rather than assuming a credential grants identical authority everywhere. Ask who can prepare or submit the required documents and which portions of the project fall outside the proposed provider’s scope.

Specify deliverables and coordination

Identify measured drawings, layouts, specifications, purchasing lists and construction-phase services separately. Ask who coordinates structural, electrical or other specialist design when needed. AIA describes several ways to work with architects, including different relationships with contractors. Record who the provider’s client is and who answers questions when design and construction information conflict.

Use a rental-specific design example

Suppose the owner wants a new sleeping layout and a refreshed interior. First determine whether the proposal changes building configuration or regulated use; then assign the necessary professional review. Separately develop furniture dimensions, durable finish selections and replacement information. Attractive renderings should not be treated as proof that the resulting layout is approved or suitable for the proposed occupancy.

Clarify purchasing and installation

Ask who places orders, holds warranties, receives deliveries and checks substitutions. Record markups or purchasing fees and who accepts items that differ from specifications. If the contractor discovers a field condition, route the question to the designated design lead. Avoid informal substitutions that change clearances or system requirements without the appropriate review.

Accept a complete handoff

Before final payment, collect the agreed drawings, finish schedule, product information and unresolved-items list. Give the operator usable care and replacement details without treating them as a substitute for construction records. Evaluate each provider against its contract and qualified scope. Neither design service guarantees rental revenue or removes the need for required local approvals. Schedule a final walkthrough focused on the agreed deliverables. Record substitutions and missing information so the operator does not have to reconstruct purchasing decisions after the design team leaves.

Prepare the evidence and next action

  • Project change and constraint list
  • Local qualification and submission check
  • Named design coordination lead
  • Purchasing and substitution authority
  • Final drawings and product handoff
