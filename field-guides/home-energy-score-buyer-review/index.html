Read a Home Energy Score in an STR Purchase File

AI-assisted editorial guide · Published November 20, 2026

The U.S. Department of Energy’s Home Energy Score provides a report on a home’s energy characteristics and potential improvements. A buyer can use that report to ask better questions about a property. It should not be treated as a forecast of short-term-rental utility bills or as a substitute for a building inspection and actual operating records.

Confirm the report belongs to this property

Record the address, assessment date and the assessor information shown in the report. Ask for the complete document rather than a screenshot of the score. Check whether material changes occurred afterward. A report prepared before an equipment replacement or renovation may not describe the property now being offered.

Read the assumptions and supporting facts

The DOE describes the report as including the score, facts about the home and recommendations. Review those parts together. Identify which building characteristics support the assessment and ask the assessor about unclear items. A single number is difficult to use responsibly when its basis and limitations have been separated from the report.

Separate standardized estimates from guest use

A rental’s actual energy use depends on its operating pattern, equipment and other circumstances. Do not substitute a standardized report estimate for documented bills without explaining the difference. Keep actual costs and the report in separate columns. Ask a qualified professional to help interpret material discrepancies instead of adjusting numbers until they match.

Review recommendations as proposed work

An improvement recommendation is not a contractor quote or a guaranteed saving. Obtain scope and cost information from qualified providers where work is being considered. Ask about dependencies, installation requirements and whether further evaluation is needed. Keep potential savings as an assumption until there is an appropriate basis for using them in a decision.

Connect the findings to buyer priorities

Identify which questions matter before purchase and which can be addressed later. For example, a concern about equipment condition may need inspection, while a possible efficiency upgrade may remain optional. Do not let a favorable score obscure another material issue. The report should inform the broader diligence file rather than determine the purchase on its own.

Use a comparison example carefully

Suppose two properties have different scores but very different sizes and amenity packages. Comparing only the score could mislead the buyer about likely bills. Review the underlying facts, available utility records and proposed operations. Record what can reasonably be compared and what remains uncertain before using the report to prioritize additional professional questions. Preserve the source report so another reviewer can follow the same reasoning and identify changed assumptions.

Prepare the evidence and next action

  • Complete report and assessment date
  • Supporting building facts
  • Actual utility records kept separate
  • Qualified scope for proposed improvements
  • Documented limits of any comparison
